This roundup circles one question that AI has made sharper: when code, and often the tests too, come from agents, how do we know the software does what it should? Alongside practical advice on scaling Playwright, contract testing and deciding how much to automate, there are candid pieces on burnout, shrinking timelines and how the tester's role is changing.

## Scaling Playwright Suites Without Losing Trust
Oliver Stenbom of Endform talks about growing a Playwright suite from a few tests to hundreds, and why flakiness usually points to genuine application defects rather than noise. The episode shows how repeating one test 100 times in parallel exposed a race condition, which an agent then diagnosed using MCP and OpenTelemetry traces. It also explains how Lovable got about 300 tests to finish in under two minutes per pull request, and it names test isolation as the first thing to fix. [Original on TestGuild Automation Podcast](https://testtalks.libsyn.com/playwright-at-scale-run-500-tests-in-minutes)

## Pushing Back on Forced AI Adoption
This essay criticizes mandated AI rollouts and connects them to rising anxiety, burnout and job insecurity among tech workers, drawing on APA findings. It also borrows the term 'workslop' for AI output that looks polished but has little substance by the time it reaches leadership. The author's case is that healthy skepticism belongs in any responsible tool adoption, which is familiar ground for people whose job is to question things. [Original on Quality Remarks](https://qualityremarks.com/main-3k-character-syndrome/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=main-3k-character-syndrome)

## Keeping Tests as Data So Agents Can't Rewrite the Contract
Matteo Vaccari argues that when agents write both the implementation and its tests, developers effectively become the customer. That makes customer-level acceptance tests the main agreement between people and the AI. Storing that agreement as readable test data rather than code makes it harder for an agent to change it quietly, and AI now makes building custom DSLs and test runners cheap enough to be practical. [Original on Martin Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/tests-as-data.html)

## Common Hurdles When Adopting Contract Testing
Bas Dijkstra has spent years teaching and consulting on contract testing, and here describes the problems that keep coming up. The first is a conceptual one: teams used to deploying everything into a shared environment and testing it as a whole often struggle to see how contract testing differs. It is a useful reality check for anyone planning to introduce contract testing across distributed services. [Original on On Test Automation (Bas Dijkstra)](https://www.ontestautomation.com/contract-testing-implementation-challenges/)

## Can One AI Agent Really Review Another?
This Polish post asks whether a green pipeline plus approval from a second AI reviewer gives you any independent assurance. It cites an ESEM 2026 study covering more than 248,000 pull requests that were both written and reviewed with AI involvement, about 45,000 of them reviewed by a different AI product. The warning is that both agents can share the same mistaken assumptions or lack business context, so passing checks are not enough to justify a merge. [Original on jaktestowac.pl](https://jaktestowac.pl/ai-code-review-weryfikacja-kodu/)

## Moving From Test Management to Quality Management Systems
Melissa Fisher and Rosie Sherry discuss what software teams can borrow from quality management systems, which grew out of manufacturing and ISO 9001. Melissa organizes QMS into planning, control and continuous improvement, and suggests regularly mapping where a team's work runs into friction. She also explains how the level of product risk should shape the system, a useful frame for testers who want to improve processes rather than only check outputs. [Original on Into the MoTaverse (Ministry of Testing)](https://www.ministryoftesting.com/podcasts/into-the-motaverse?wchannelid=b2j0jiwz2n&wmediaid=mkewg9sohz)

## How Much Testing Should Actually Be Automated?
Based on cost models from the ISTQB CTAL-TAE syllabus, this Polish article argues that automation pays off best somewhere around 50–60% of tests. Past that point maintenance costs climb steeply, and close to 80% the benefit disappears and automation begins to cost money. The article gives testers concrete arguments against chasing full automation, especially for scenarios that rarely run. [Original on testerzy.pl](https://testerzy.pl/baza-wiedzy/artykuly/jaki-procent-testow-nalezy-automatyzowac)

## Don't Trust AI-Written Tests Just Because They Pass
Tests produced by Codex or Claude often just capture whatever the code does now, not what it is supposed to do. The post explains why a passing suite of that kind proves little and how to check whether the tests reflect the intended behavior. It is especially relevant for teams leaning on coding assistants for test coverage. [Original on dev.to](https://dev.to/nikolay_chernev_a67479931/codex-or-claude-wrote-my-tests-how-do-i-actually-trust-them-2ooi)

## Joy, Burnout and Choosing Not to Use AI
Louise Woodhams, automation and test lead at ZapMap, talks about the small habits that make work enjoyable and about a stressful late-night incident caused by a critical PHP security fix. Louise also explains why the testing team decided not to use AI and what it costs testers when developers start asking an assistant instead of them. It offers a human counterweight to the steady push for automation everywhere. ## An Introduction to TPI NEXT
This Polish article introduces Sogeti's TPI NEXT model for assessing how mature a test process is and improving it in deliberate steps. Its maturity matrix covers 16 key areas, 4 maturity levels and 157 checkpoints, and helps teams decide which improvements to tackle first. It is a good starting point for anyone who needs a structured way to audit testing in their organization.
